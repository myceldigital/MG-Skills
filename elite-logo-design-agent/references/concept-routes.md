# Concept Route Patterns

Use these patterns to create distinct logo directions. Do not use all of them by default. Select the routes that fit the brand problem.

## 1. Pure Symbol
A standalone mark that can eventually identify without the name.

Best for:
- brands with strong ambition and repeated exposure
- apps, products, communities, hospitality, consumer goods

Risks:
- may be too abstract early
- needs strong system and repetition

Elite requirement:
- unmistakable silhouette
- simple enough to redraw
- one dominant idea

## 2. Wordmark
The brand name itself becomes the identity through custom lettering or typographic treatment.

Best for:
- distinctive names
- premium services
- consultancies
- brands where clarity and memorability of name matter most

Risks:
- default font feeling
- weak distinctiveness if not customized

Elite requirement:
- custom details that are meaningful but not gimmicky
- excellent kerning and optical spacing
- type personality aligned with brand tone

## 3. Monogram
Initials become a compact mark.

Best for:
- long names
- professional services
- fashion, hospitality, architecture, investment, clubs

Risks:
- many monograms look alike
- can become decorative but meaningless

Elite requirement:
- initials must fuse into a distinctive form, not merely overlap
- silhouette must work without explanation

## 4. Emblem or Badge
A contained identity form, often circular, shield-like, or seal-like.

Best for:
- heritage, community, clubs, food, craft, outdoors, institutions

Risks:
- can become too detailed or nostalgic
- often poor at small sizes

Elite requirement:
- simplify aggressively
- make a responsive small-use variant
- avoid unnecessary dates, slogans, and micro-detail

## 5. Abstract Mark
A nonliteral shape expresses a concept like movement, connection, precision, shelter, or transformation.

Best for:
- technology, finance, infrastructure, science, consulting, platforms

Risks:
- can become generic corporate geometry
- weak emotional connection

Elite requirement:
- clear conceptual anchor
- distinctive geometry or counterform
- avoid overused nodes, loops, hexagons, arrows, and gradients unless truly ownable

## 6. Metaphoric Object
A real-world object is reduced into a symbol with broader meaning.

Best for:
- consumer, food, nature, culture, education, publishing, wellness, travel

Risks:
- literalness
- stock-icon resemblance

Elite requirement:
- object must transform into a brand idea, not remain an illustration
- one clever reduction or unexpected formal move

## 7. Negative-Space Idea
The hidden or secondary shape reinforces the core promise.

Best for:
- delivery, movement, problem-solving, transformation, dual meanings

Risks:
- puzzle-first design
- fragile small-size gaps

Elite requirement:
- primary mark works before the hidden idea is noticed
- hidden form remains clean at intended sizes

## 8. Frame or Container
A simple device frames content and becomes a flexible system.

Best for:
- media, travel, publishing, photography, directories, curators, marketplaces

Risks:
- too minimal to own
- generic rectangle or circle

Elite requirement:
- container has distinctive proportion, corner behavior, interruption, or usage logic
- identity system proves its value

## 9. Gesture Mark
A single stroke or movement becomes the mark.

Best for:
- sport, performance, creative, speed, energy, personal brands

Risks:
- can look like a generic swoosh
- hard to own without exact proportions

Elite requirement:
- unique tension, angle, taper, rhythm, or implied motion
- extremely disciplined reproduction rules

## 10. Civic or Wayfinding Mark
A mark designed for public utility, navigation, or repeated environmental use.

Best for:
- places, events, transport, venues, campuses, districts, infrastructure

Risks:
- over-systematized and sterile
- too dependent on signage context

Elite requirement:
- instant legibility
- strong color and shape coding
- works as sign, map label, icon, and identity
